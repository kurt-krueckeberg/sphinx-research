## A. Historical Overview and Notes on Their Use

**By Volkmar Drese**

Church registers—also called *Matriken*, *matricula*, or *catalogus* in
Catholic regions, particularly in southern Germany and Austria—record the
ecclesiastical acts performed by the locally responsible pastor, especially
baptisms, marriages, and burials. In general, for the period before the
introduction of civil registration, they constitute the official registers
of births and deaths as well as marriages.

Under §73 of the Civil Status Act of February 6, 1875, even after that law
took effect, the authorities and officials who had previously been
entrusted with maintaining civil-status registers or church registers
retained “the authority and obligation … to issue certificates concerning
births, marriages, and deaths entered before this law took effect.”

In addition to ecclesiastical acts involving members of the particular
congregation, the registers frequently recorded family events affecting all
residents of a parish district, including members of other
denominations—sometimes without indicating that they belonged to another
faith. Conversely, members of religious communities that had no parish of
their own in a locality—for example, Catholics or members of the Reformed
Church in Lutheran places, and vice versa—must often be sought in the
registers of neighboring churches belonging to their own denomination.

During wartime, the registers may also contain entries concerning people
from the countryside who took refuge in nearby towns. Special registers
were frequently maintained for members of the military establishment, even
when the troops were not permanently stationed in one place. Separate
church registers also sometimes existed for members of the narrower or
broader court establishment at princely residences, for members of the
general patronage families, and even for university congregations, such as
Erlangen.

The Protestant church registers of Germany were created primarily as a
result of the territorial church ordinances of the sixteenth century.
Catholic registers are generally based on a rule introduced by the Council
of Trent in 1563 requiring every parish to keep a baptismal register and a
marriage register. Detailed rules concerning death registers did not appear
until the *Rituale Romanum* of Pope Paul V in 1614.

Many comments important for the history of church registers in the
individual German territories can be found in the introductions to various
inventories. The sources surviving from before the Thirty Years’ War are
admittedly only a very small portion of the original records. As with that
war, fires, personal negligence by those responsible for keeping the
records, and especially the wartime and postwar events of the present
century have caused losses.

The church registers—which in Protestant regions were written in German, in
Catholic regions often in Latin until well into the nineteenth century, and
in congregations of religious exiles such as the Huguenots and Bohemian
Brethren in the language of their countries of origin—are usually kept at
the parish offices. These offices ordinarily retain the second copies and,
as a rule, the duplicate copies submitted by the superintendent’s offices.

In the Rhineland, older registers dating from before 1800 are also found at
mayoral offices, civil-registry offices, and courts. Centralization that
would protect records from improper, and sometimes even incomprehensible
and therefore hazardous, storage has so far been achieved in only a few
places. On the other hand, centralized collections face a greater danger of
wholesale destruction in the event of a disaster.

Even today, by no means all church registers have been made accessible
through alphabetical indexes arranged by surname. Up through the
seventeenth century, indexes arranged according to given or baptismal names
are also found, and these naturally have only limited value for
genealogists.

More recently, church registers have often become easier to use through a
process of indexing or “carding”—that is, by recording each entry on a
separate index card. Examples include the standardized system in the State
of Hesse and the exemplary work of the Church Register Office in Hanover
and the Church Register Office of Alt-Berlin.

In the long term, the aim of such indexing is the creation of village
family books, or *Ortssippenbücher*.

After 1933, church registers were placed under legal protection as written
cultural monuments and now belong to the broad category of cultural
property defined in a United Nations convention. That convention obligates
governments to take the measures necessary to protect such written
materials.

Unfortunately, the security microfilming begun for this purpose of church
registers surviving from their beginnings through 1800 progressed only
slowly. By contrast, the filming programs conducted by the archival
authorities themselves have continued with increasing intensity in recent
years and have already been completed in some ecclesiastical jurisdictions
and state archives.

When using church-register entries for genealogical research, the following
details must be considered.

Besides information concerning baptism and burial, information on birth and
death is not always given. One must therefore determine precisely to which
event a date refers.

Although the denomination of the baptized, married, and buried persons is
usually not explicitly recorded, it can generally be assumed with a high
degree of certainty that the persons concerned belonged to the denomination
of the parish office in which the church registers were maintained.

A newborn child often received the given name or names of a brother or
sister who had died earlier. It is therefore necessary to determine which
of several children bearing the same name survived. Even siblings alive at
the same time can sometimes be found with identical given names.

The given names of grandparents were frequently given to grandchildren and
were not uncommon. In Catholic regions in particular, the names of
godparents were often selected and passed down.

The first child of a marriage was sometimes born or baptized at the
residence of the maternal grandparents. Entries concerning godparents—and
the origin or residence of the child’s parents—can provide important
evidence of family relationships and stages in a person’s life.

Children who died shortly after birth were often entered in only one church
register—either only in the baptismal register or, like stillborn children,
only in the burial register. In earlier periods, the sex of the child may
not be stated.

Information concerning illegitimate children and foundlings was
occasionally entered in reverse order—that is, the entries were written
upside down—as were entries in death registers concerning children who died
unbaptized, suicides, men killed in duels, heretics, persons who had been
executed, and similar cases.

Special indexes were sometimes maintained for illegitimate children.
Particular attention must be paid to illegitimate families because the
assignment of surnames to illegitimate children differed from one territory
to another.

The sequence of the given names entered in the record must also be
observed. The name by which a person was commonly known was not always
indicated, as is now often done by underlining. Sometimes it is only
possible to establish that two persons were certainly identical by
comparing changes in given names at birth, marriage, and death.

At baptism, a child may be described merely as “the child N.N.” without
being given any first name at all. Even adults may be recorded without a
surname—for example, “deceased, a woman aged 82, … the musician from
Hamburg,” and similar descriptions.

Baptisms generally took place on one of the days immediately following
birth—frequently on the day of birth itself in Catholic regions. Baptism
might also be postponed somewhat to allow time to notify godparents and for
them to travel. Only among the children of the nobility was it customary to
wait for a foreign or distant godparent. Late baptism, meaning years after
birth as an adult baptism, is found among Mennonites.

Marriage must in many cases be sought in the bride’s place of residence.
Married women are sometimes listed under their father’s surname, for
example: “died, Marie Kern, wife of Heinrich Bergmann.”

Special registers are occasionally found for couples who, for some reason,
were exempted from the publication of marriage banns.

Divorced women were frequently described as widows even when the divorced
husband was still living.

The date of marriage often coincided with the groom’s admission as a master
into his guild.

In church registers, the term *Mischehen* refers to marriages between
persons of different denominations.

In addition to the date of the marriage—the marriage ceremony or
wedding—the date of the engagement is often recorded in the sixteenth and
seventeenth centuries. Under German law, the engagement was much more
legally binding than the ecclesiastical blessing.

Burial on the same day as death may indicate an epidemic, such as plague.

Ages recorded at death must be treated cautiously, even when the deceased
was born in the same parish. Such ages often rested upon unchecked oral
statements made by surviving relatives.

The absence of a reference to an ecclesiastical act or family event in a
church register is not conclusive evidence that the event did not occur. An
entry may have been omitted during wartime, epidemics, a vacancy in the
pastorate, or for various other reasons—for example, emergency baptisms
performed by laymen.

During the first days of January, the person maintaining the register may
sometimes have forgotten to change the year number.

To obtain information from church registers, when personal inspection is
not possible, one should write to the parish office in question. Personal
visits are best made by genealogists with appropriate technical
preparation, not on Sundays, feast days, or the days immediately preceding
them, and only at the parsonage itself.

When the proper parish office in a small place is unknown, the request
should be addressed:

> To the … Parish Office for Xdorf

Alternatively, one may write to the church authority responsible for the
region concerned. In larger cities with several parish offices, one may
write to any one of them and request that the inquiry be circulated among
the other parish offices if it cannot be answered there.

For church registers in ecclesiastical custody, the principle of provenance
applies in the strict sense. This means that when inquiries addressed to
parish offices or deaneries—superintendent’s offices—remain unanswered, one
should contact the archival administration of the appropriate territorial
church.

Those administrations can also provide information concerning duplicate
copies, films, and card indexes that may exist.

Finally, in special cases, it is advisable to consult genealogists working
on the local, regional, or population history of the area. From their card
indexes, copies of church registers and other sources, and lists, they may
be able to provide information.

Because the number of church registers lost during the Second World War is
not inconsiderable, this last-mentioned source of information is becoming
increasingly important.

To determine the proper territorial church or diocesan jurisdiction in an
individual case, one should use the *German Ecclesiastical Address Book*,
third edition, Berlin, 1937, for Protestant church registers. For Catholic
sources, one should use the *General Register of the Catholic Clergy of
Germany*, twelfth edition, Passau, 1934.

When necessary, these should be used together with one of the well-known
German gazetteers or the new edition of *Müller’s Great German Gazetteer*,
Wuppertal-Barmen, 1968.

Particularly valuable is the gazetteer published by R. Oehler-Neiße,
*Localities Situated under Foreign Administration*, Wuppertal-Barmen, no
date, arranged both foreign-language-to-German and
German-to-foreign-language. Knowledge of the new place-name terminology is
indispensable to researchers dealing with local offices in the formerly
German eastern territories.

The addresses of the territorial-church archival administrations are, in
most cases, identical with those of the territorial church offices. In
cities that are seats of Catholic bishops, inquiries should be addressed to
the diocesan administrations.

One should, however, always write first to the appropriate parish office,
even when it may be assumed that the church registers are no longer located
at their place of origin.

This principle does not apply to former German imperial territories under
Polish or Soviet sovereignty. In such cases, inquiries should be addressed
to the Protestant Central Archive in Berlin, Jebensstraße 3, 1000 Berlin
12, Charlottenburg.

This also applies to places in the former ecclesiastical province of
Brandenburg, including the Neumark and Lower Lusatia, because the
territorial-church archive there is now administered as an independent
archival body.

When seeking Catholic sources, one should contact the Catholic Church
Register Office for Expellees, Bavariaring 24, 8000 Munich 2.

The rules adopted by the individual territorial churches and diocesan
jurisdictions govern the use of church registers, as do the provisions of
Catholic canon law, with only minor variations, and the special
instructions contained in territorial church-register regulations.

Church registers may not, as a general rule, be lent out. Sending them
elsewhere for consultation is likewise prohibited.

Trading in church registers, as well as acquiring them, is a criminal
offense. Privately held church registers that appear outside their proper
locality must be surrendered to the responsible authorities. Information
can be provided by territorial church offices and ordinariates.

The question of fees, which formerly often caused disagreements, is now
regulated almost uniformly. At present, fee schedules normally apply that
were newly established in a framework directive of the Protestant Church in
Germany dated April 10, 1978, in a revised version dated September 17,
1982.

These fees benefit the local church treasury or the archival
administrations and are intended to finance the repair and preservation of
church registers, including photocopying, microfilming, and the preparation
of indexes.

When making written inquiries, it is advisable to limit in advance the
amount that may be spent—for example:

> I ask that the cost initially not exceed DM 10—[or DM 20].

For informal, uncertified information from church registers—when legally
valid certificates are not required, for example for pedigree charts or
ancestor lists—no document fees are payable. Only the customary search fees
must be paid.

The value or lack of value of information supplied by a parish office
depends not only upon the personal conscientiousness of the person
providing it, but to a considerable extent upon that person’s greater or
lesser expertise in handling handwritten texts from the sixteenth through
eighteenth centuries.

Conditions in this respect have improved considerably as a result of the
increasing centralization of church archives. On the other hand, it must be
observed that the ability—and also the willingness—of local pastors to
decipher handwritten texts of the sixteenth through eighteenth centuries
has shown a marked decline in recent years.

The person responsible for a church register is probably liable under §73
of the Civil Status Act of February 6, 1875, for providing incorrect
information—that is, for violating an official public duty.

Such liability apparently does not apply when a clergyman, out of special
interest, voluntary helpfulness, or similar motives, has conducted
extensive research based on the church registers and provided information
in the form of a complete pedigree chart or similar compilation.

The right to receive information from church registers, or to inspect them,
is no longer disputed today. It must nevertheless be expressly pointed out
that church-register custodians—pastors and their staff, church-register
offices, and territorial church archival administrations—are not required
to conduct extensive genealogical research.

For users’ personal inspection of church registers from the more recent
past, the standard closure periods applying to archival material containing
personal information apply, generally fifty years.

In addition to the church registers themselves, parish offices not
infrequently possess other records that may be considered ecclesiastical
sources for genealogical research.

These include family or soul registers compiled from church registers,
burial registers and so-called bell registers that served as replacements
or predecessors of death registers, recording the ringing of church bells
for especially solemn burials or weddings—for example, in Greifswald,
Nuremberg, and Uelzen.

There are also lists of persons formerly confirmed—who in Reformed regions
might be as old as twenty—lists of communicants, evening-service attendees,
sometimes arranged in complete family groups, memorial services for the
dead, and lists of converts—that is, persons who transferred to another
religious community.

Additional supplements to church registers include:

* lists of marriage banns, also called *Sponsalienverzeichnisse*;
* proclamation books or slips from which the clergyman announced from the
  pulpit the official acts performed during the preceding week;
* cemetery registers and church-pew registers, which may serve as
  substitutes for lost church registers;
* church accounts, including poor-relief and alms accounts, which may
  contain valuable details concerning displaced refugees, burned-out
  persons, discharged soldiers, and wounded soldiers;
* marriage dispensations;
* records and accounts concerning foundations and stipends under ecclesiastical supervision.

In Reformed congregations, presbytery minutes are also an important source for individual cases of church discipline and divorce. Even in Protestant territories, divorces remained under the authority of consistories acting as ecclesiastical marriage courts until the nineteenth century.
