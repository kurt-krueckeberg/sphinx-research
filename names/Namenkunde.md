# German Onomastics: *Rufnamen* and *Familiennamen*

## Citation

“Namenkunde,” in Taschenbuch für Familiengeschichtsforschung, ed. Wolfgang
Ribbe and Eckart Henning, 10th ed. (Neustadt an der Aisch: Verlag Degener &
Co., 1990), 344-350

**Translation note:** The German terms *Rufname*, *Familienname*,
*Beiname*, and *Übername* are retained throughout the translation. At
their first occurrence, they are followed by an English explanation. In
this chapter, *Rufname* is used broadly for a personal name or forename
in contrast to a *Familienname*; it does not necessarily identify which
of several baptismal names was actually used as a person’s everyday name.
*Beiname* (additional identifying name) refers to a name added to
distinguish a person and may indicate origin, residence, occupation,
parentage, or another association. *Übername* (descriptive identifying
name, nickname, or epithet) is a more specific type of *Beiname* based
on a personal characteristic, appearance, conduct, or reputation.

## Onomastics

Although sources for family history often extend back only a few
centuries, *Familiennamen* (family names) have their roots in earlier times. The name
has therefore been called, with some justification, the oldest evidence
of family history. It can provide information about the occupation,
homeland, or appearance of ancestors at the time when family names 
became fixed. Above all, the linguistic form of names—especially names
derived from places of origin and occupations—as well as their sounds
and word formation generally permit conclusions about the area in which
they were distributed and thus about the origin of a name-bearer’s
ancestors. Much the same applies to *Rufnamen* (personal names), which
varied in their regional distribution and also developed differently within
particular spheres of religious influence. Here too, dialect forms can
sometimes be observed that permit conclusions about origin. Other names
have been extensively reshaped, usually through migration, because they
were adapted, for various reasons, to a new environment. Finally, one of
the most frequent causes of a change in a name is a change in spelling,
which remained permissible well into the nineteenth century.

## *Rufnamen*

Historically, the *Rufname* (personal name or forename) is older than the *Familienname*. The use of
a single name continued far into the Middle Ages, because life took
place in small, manageable communities in which one name was sufficient
to distinguish one person from another. German personal names of that
period were borrowed from the Germanic vocabulary or came from the
Christian—that is, foreign-language—sphere.

The older stratum consists of Germanic theophoric names. In bestowing a
name, people probably wished to transfer to the child the qualities
expressed by it or to bind those qualities to the child. Animal names,
weapon names, and tribal or ethnic names belong in this context.
Christianization gradually displaced the Germanic style of naming.
Christian names, especially Hebrew names but also names of Greek, Latin,
Romance, and other origin, took their place. By the end of the twelfth
century, names from the New Testament predominated. Christian naming
spread beyond tribal and ethnic boundaries, but it also created new
regional peculiarities. In addition to social estates, guilds, and
trades, tribes and “countries” also adopted patron saints—for example,
Saint Rupert in Bavaria, Saint Florian in Austria, and Saint Kilian in
Main Franconia. Promoted especially by the Franciscans and Dominicans,
patronage by a saint became increasingly important as patronage in
naming. The Christian names adopted in this way were generally
Germanized, as happened analogously in other languages (Latin Jacobus =
German Jakob = French Jacques = English James = Italian Giacomo).

With Romance culture, foreign secular *Rufnamen* also entered Germany.
The reception of French romances had a particularly lasting effect.
During the Middle Ages there was probably no ecclesiastical influence
favoring an emphatically Christian choice of names. Only when the
Reformers rejected saints’ names and wished to permit only biblical
*Rufnamen* did the Church make the saints’ names its own. The Council of
Trent (1545–1563) expressly rejected “heathen” baptismal names and
required Christian ones.

The distribution of these Christian names shows both a west–east
gradient and a south–north gradient, together with a contrast between
town and countryside. A general leveling did not occur until the
fifteenth century. A social gradient can also be observed. The leading
strata—princes, nobility, and the patriciate—often led the way in
adopting foreign *Rufnamen*. Under the influence of mendicant orders,
Christian *Rufnamen* spread especially rapidly among the urban middle
class. Particular names rose and fell in popularity according to region
and period. For the genealogist it is therefore especially valuable to
trace the history of individual *Rufnamen* and to investigate the
reasons for their appearance, decline, or complete absence at particular
times and in particular regions.

In their struggle against the cult of the saints, Protestants replaced
saints’ names with Old Testament names; Calvinists even permitted only
biblical names. In opposition, the Synod of Tournai in 1574 prohibited
the adoption of such names. Yet Old Testament *Rufnamen* survived beyond
the eighteenth century only in orthodox currents of German Protestantism
and among Puritans, Presbyterians, and Mennonites.

Under the influence of Protestantism, German *Rufnamen* gradually gained
ground. Johann Fischart, for example, promoted them in his
Geschichtsklitterung, chapter 10. From that time onward the
arts—especially poetry—proved to be important trendsetters in naming.
Fischart also wished to enrich the German stock of names through
independent coinages. He is therefore regarded as a forerunner of the
seventeenth-century linguistic-purist movement, represented by such
figures as Moscherosch and Philipp von Zesen. Zesen translated classical
names into German (Dorothea = Himmelshulde; Philipp = Marhold) and
reduced French names to what he regarded as their supposed original form
(Louise = Ludwiche).

More successful were the new creations of German Protestantism.
Following the model of old German names such as Gotthard and Siegfried,
new multi-part wish names with a religious basis were formed, including
Gnadenfried and Liebfried. Similarly, Timotheus was rendered as
Fürchtegott, Amadeus as Gottlieb, and Adam as Erdmann. Enthusiasm for
these Protestant-style inventions, to which Catholicism was not entirely
opposed, soon subsided, however, around 1800.

In its choice of *Rufnamen*, Catholic Germany has remained faithful to
medieval tradition down to the present, although at various times
nonreligious names and a number of foreign saints’ names were also able
to gain a foothold. The latter development probably goes back to
religious orders that sought to establish recognition for their own
saints. This was already true in the Middle Ages, when the Friars Minor
spread the names Franz and Anton, while the Dominicans promoted
Dominicus with less success. The Jesuits promoted Ignaz, Xaver, and
Alois.

Beginning in the late eighteenth century, models from political and
social life—above all territorial rulers—and from belles-lettres
increasingly influenced naming. As a result, Italian, Spanish, French,
English, Russian, and Scandinavian *Rufnamen* spread in Germany
independently of ecclesiastical influence. The Italian opera and the
Spanish romance provided impulses in the seventeenth century; enthusiasm
for English literature did so in the eighteenth; and Russian and Nordic
poetry did so in the later nineteenth. *Rufnamen* from German literary
works also became fashionable for a time, including Goethe’s Lotte,
Schiller’s Johanna, and figures from Richard Wagner’s operas such as
Eva, Elsa, and Siegfried. Naming in the nineteenth and twentieth
centuries was influenced even more strongly by popular literature—for
example Marlitt’s Reichsgräfin Gisela and Meyer-Förster’s play
Alt-Heidelberg, which gave rise to Karl-Heinz. After the Second World
War, film heroes and popular singers chiefly occupied this role.

double *Rufnamen* have been widespread since the sixteenth century,
although they were by no means unknown earlier, as the
thirteenth-century Wolfdietrich legend shows. The practice probably came
to Germany from France, although such forms can already be observed in
Germanic times. At first it was practiced only by the nobility; it then
spread gradually from southern and southwestern Germany toward the
north. In the seventeenth century the middle class and the rural
population also adopted it. The practice declined again in the
eighteenth century, although several baptismal names continued to be
given. In the second half of the nineteenth century it revived, as in
Rudolf Alexander Schröder. At times the number of *Rufnamen* increased
extravagantly—to as many as twenty—causing the Church to take measures
against such excess. Besides ostentation, other factors also contributed
to the rise of double *Rufnamen*: a child might be given, in addition to
his or her own name, the name of the father or mother, a godparent, or a
particular saint.

German *Rufnamen* were therefore chosen anew in every generation. In
addition to fashion and regional criteria, sociological and
psychological considerations played a significant role in the choice of
a name. The contribution of the various social groups to the stock of
*Rufnamen* was always unequal, although names did pass from one social
group to another. Thus, as Adolf Bach observed, the modern stock of
*Rufnamen* is an image and expression of the intellectual history of the
German people, both in its unity and in its divisions. It reflects the
conflict among indigenous Germanic, Christian-ecclesiastical, and
classical traditions: what was originally native first gave way, but
everything that has lived in the history of the people appears again, in
later reflection, in the stock of *Rufnamen*.

## *Familiennamen*

The custom of identifying a son as belonging to his father by means of
the father’s name—a patronymic—also goes back to Germanic times. The
title character of the Hildebrandslied calls himself Hiltibrant,
Heribrantes sunu (“Hildebrand, Heribrand’s son”), and calls his son
Hadubrant, Hiltibrantes sunu. There were also special clan names,
particularly among ruling families; among other things, the *Rufname* of
an ancestor was used for this purpose.

Alongside patronymics and clan names there were *Beinamen* (additional identifying names), which must
not be equated with *Familiennamen* despite their similarities.
*Rufnamen* themselves can be documented as *Beinamen*. Some *Beinamen*
indicate origin or descent; others identify a place of residence. They
may also designate occupation or kinship, or they may be *Übernamen* or
*Übernamen*. Some were one-time additions used to identify a particular
person; others were permanent personal *Beinamen* or hereditary
*Familiennamen*. Patronymics, clan names, names of origin, and
*Übernamen* belong to the latter category as soon as they ceased to
refer only to one person and became hereditary. The recurrence of the
same occupation or physical resemblance may have facilitated hereditary
transmission. A name is also considered inherited when siblings bear the
same *Beiname*; in occupational names, when the name contrasts with the
occupation actually practiced; and sometimes when the addition dictus
(“called”) indicates that a hereditary *Familienname* already existed.

fixed *Familiennamen* can be documented in northern Italian cities from
the eighth and ninth centuries. After the collapse of the Roman naming
system during the Migration Period, a new system had developed. fixed
*Beinamen* appear in southern France in the tenth century and in
French-speaking Switzerland from the eleventh. The development spread to
Germany. *Familiennamen* in the proper sense gradually became customary
there from the twelfth century onward. Beginning in the west and south,
they had become established in most German territories by about 1600. In
Friesland, however, hereditary *Familiennamen* appeared later and were
introduced only through official compulsion.

Once the use of two names had become generally customary, the older
single-name form was retained chiefly by persons of lower status,
especially servants, journeymen, farmhands, and maids. Despite the rise
of *Familiennamen*, the *Rufname* retained for centuries a prominence
that it no longer has today. Even people of higher rank usually
addressed one another by their *Rufnamen* for a long time. In the
fifteenth and sixteenth centuries tax lists sometimes recorded only
*Rufnamen*, and Luther’s contemporaries seldom called him D. Martinus;
his followers in the sixteenth century likewise called themselves
Martinists. The pupil lists of the Bamberg school were arranged by the
pupils’ *Rufnamen* into the eighteenth century, and the Prague
matriculation book even favored the *Rufname* until 1776. Dürer’s
monogram, with its large D extending over the A, shows that his
*Rufname* was more important to him than his *Familienname*.

Princes and high ecclesiastical dignitaries, with the exception of
cardinals, still use only their *Rufnamen* today—for example, Prince
Louis Ferdinand and Pope Paul. By contrast, in the eighteenth century it
was entirely customary, even in familiar circles, to use
*Familiennamen*. In some German regions, at least until about 1900,
wives addressed their husbands by their *Familiennamen*.

The cities led the way in adopting and consistently using
*Familiennamen*. In the countryside, where bureaucratic influence was
weaker, they remained uncommon longer. Even today, unofficial house and
farm names are sometimes used in rural areas to identify families; these
names may be inherited through generations. In substance, house and farm
names fall into the same categories of meaning as official
*Familiennamen*. In Siegerland, for example, most such names developed
from *Rufnamen*. Normally, the name proceeds from the farm, as among
Westphalian and Hanoverian peasants and also in Bavaria and Tyrol.
Whoever married into or purchased a farm adopted its name, as in
Langhof-Bauer. Even the name of a long-extinct owner family was
continued and transferred to later owners. Craftsmen and officials in
the countryside were often called by the name of their occupation, which
their children then also bore. In towns, house names became prominent
especially from the thirteenth century onward, although not uniformly in
every area, since the number of *Familiennamen* derived from house names
depends on the local custom of naming houses.

Leaving aside patronymics and house and farm names, fixed
*Familiennamen* may be classified according to whether they were formed
from personal names, places of origin, occupations, and so forth. The
frequency of these categories varies by region and period.
*Familiennamen* formed from Germanic *Rufnamen* belong to the oldest
stratum. From the later thirteenth century onward, *Familiennamen* based
on foreign *Rufnamen* also appeared to a lesser extent, although they
constituted a substantial share only in the Rhineland—for example Görres
from Gregorius. *Familiennamen* derived from *Rufnamen* are especially
frequent in the western Low German region and comparatively rare in
Swabia.

The growth of towns, and above all medieval German eastward settlement,
was associated with extensive internal migration that encouraged the
formation of *Familiennamen* based on places of origin. In a new place
of residence, a person’s origin was better known than his name. Even in
the nineteenth century, traveling journeymen were given *Beinamen* based
on their places of origin. *Familiennamen* based on residence are
generally considered liable to change among the nobility; among peasants
they occur chiefly in areas of farmstead settlement.

occupational *Familiennamen* developed in connection with the growth of
towns, although occupational terms appearing in early documents were at
first neither actual occupational descriptions nor *Familiennamen*. Such
terms derived from *Übernamen* often appear in the written record only
after names of origin and occupational names, and they gained particular
importance only in the late phase of family-name formation, during the
fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, under the influence of coarse popular
humor. Sentence-like names such as Packan and Luginsloch are known in
Germany from the twelfth century, became popular in the fifteenth, and
occur especially in Bavaria, Austria, and Lower Saxony.

Redundancy, or pleonasm, in *Familiennamen* is common. In some cases it
disappeared; in others it arose anew. Double forms with genannt
(“called”) are still familiar today, as in Schmoll genannt Eisenwerth.
Closely related to pleonasm is the alternation of *Beinamen*.

Until the seventeenth century it was often only the *Rufname* that
remained stable. Changes of *Familienname*, by contrast, occurred in
every social estate. Noblemen frequently changed their *Beiname* along
with their property, so that members of the same family could bear
different names based on their residences. It should also be noted that
the noble particle von became customary only gradually. Alongside it was
the bourgeois indication of origin von X…, which did not imply
ennoblement. From the nineteenth century onward, bourgeois private
citizens with a fully written von were customarily so designated,
whereas the abbreviation v. was reserved for old nobility certified by
the heraldic office. Bourgeois families often changed their names
together with the houses in which they lived.

Johannes Gutenberg, for example, was actually named Henne Gensfleisch.
He bore the name familiar to us from his mother’s house in Mainz.
Peasant families identified by farm names likewise underwent a change of
name when the farm they cultivated changed, including when they married
into another farm.

Beginning in the seventeenth century, changes of *Familienname* were
prohibited in Germany—in Bavaria in 1677, Austria in 1767, and Prussia
in 1794. The vocabulary that arose in the sixteenth century, much of it
drawn from the military sphere, such as Profittlich and Kutscher,
nevertheless continued to influence the formation of *Familiennamen*.
The expansion of the vocabulary since the seventeenth century has been
largely irrelevant in this respect. fixed *Familiennamen* survived least
securely in northwestern Germany. Until the middle of the nineteenth
century the Hanoverian government ordered the preparation of lists of
*Familiennamen* for East Friesland because their permanence was still
not assured. In Lippe, the stabilization of *Familiennamen* did not
prevail until about 1865.

From ancient times the Frisians used *Beinamen* that changed from
generation to generation and were formed from the father’s *Rufname*. A
child’s stem-name was the father’s *Rufname*, and the father’s stem-name
was used as the eldest son’s *Rufname*, so that the eldest grandson
always bore the same name as his grandfather. This is patronymic name
formation. From the eighteenth century onward, *Familiennamen* derived
from place-names and occupations also became increasingly common in
Friesland, but patronymic naming was not completely abandoned. The
result was a three-name system comparable to the Russian system.

Despite general administrative efforts to make *Familiennamen*
permanent, exceptions have always existed. At the end of the nineteenth
century, Polish, Masurian, and Lithuanian *Familiennamen* in the
Rhenish-Westphalian industrial region were adapted to German. Because
bearers of common names were difficult to distinguish from one another,
new names were created. The *Familienname* of the Luther biographer
Schottmüller was formed from those of his parents: his father was Müller
and his mother, née Schott. A place of residence was also frequently
incorporated into the *Familienname*, as in Schmidt-Vockenhausen.

The addition of a wife’s name to a *Familienname* appears more
frequently only from 1830 onward and seems initially to have become
customary among merchants and major industrialists. It first achieved
full official recognition in Switzerland. Double names were also adopted
to preserve the name of a family dying out in the male line. The name
Krupp von Bohlen und Halbach was created in 1906, when Gustav von Bohlen
und Halbach married Alfred Krupp’s granddaughter and no male heirs
existed in the Krupp family.

Relatively early it became customary for noble families to name
themselves after their estates or to add the name of an estate to their
*Familienname*, as in von Bismarck-Schönhausen. To distinguish bearers
of common bourgeois *Familiennamen*, the name of the person’s birthplace
or residence was sometimes added, as in Hoffmann von Fallersleben. When
people were ennobled, new names—sometimes invented forms—were often
created, as in Hofmann von Hofmannswaldau. These must be distinguished
from honorific *Beinamen* granted to victorious commanders during the
Wars of Liberation, such as Yorck von Wartenburg. At the same time,
names have always disappeared, whether through the extinction of
families or the elimination of offensive *Familiennamen*.

In the Middle Ages and the early modern period, German names were
translated into Latin, Greek, and sometimes Hebrew, or assimilated to
those languages. Monks bore Latin *Beinamen*, some of them translations
of corresponding German names, such as Notger balbulus, labio, and
teutonicus. Humanists translated their names into Latin and Greek.
Because the meaning of German names was often no longer understood,
mistranslations were unavoidable, as in Konrad Bickel becoming Conradus
Celtes.

The Roman three-name system also returned to fashion. Celtes coined the
phrase Poetas esse trinomines (“poets should have three names”) and
accordingly called himself Conradus Celtes Protucius. Occasionally more
than three name elements occur, as in Aureolus Theophrastus Bombastus
Paracelsus ab Hohenheim. University and school matriculation records
often entered the student’s or pupil’s place of origin as the third
element of such names, as in Engelbert Wusterwitz van Brandenburg.

Classicizing forms produced by attaching an ending and using foreign
stress are often found, sometimes alternating with a translated name.
The Brandenburg pastor and historiographer Andreas Engel used both his
German name with an added Latin ending, Engelius, and the Latin
translation Angelus. In names of origin containing von, that word was
usually rendered by Latin a or ab, as in Thomas a Kempis. Many names
created by translation or classicization did not survive permanently;
others remain in use today.

Among *Familiennamen* of foreign origin that became customary in
Germany, the numerically largest group consists of names of Polish,
Wendish, Lithuanian, Masurian, and Prussian origin. Most were adopted
during medieval German eastward settlement and expansion. French and
Italian names in Germany are borne by descendants of Huguenots expelled
from France and by other emigrants, as well as by descendants of Italian
artists, craftsmen, and merchants who settled in Germany over the past
centuries. Their share of the German stock of *Familiennamen* is small.
Spanish, Scottish, English, Nordic, Russian, Arabic, Turkish, and other
*Familiennamen* occur only occasionally.

## Onomastics and Family-History Research

This brief survey shows that *Familiennamen* underwent a complex and
many-layered development.

An important prerequisite for interpreting a *Familienname* is precise
knowledge of the changes that the individual name underwent over time
and of its geographic distribution. Such knowledge usually depends on
constructing a pedigree extending far back in time. Establishing that
pedigree must therefore be the first objective. From all available
sources, one should collect everything that can be determined about
changes in the form of the name. Once the oldest form of the name in the
historical record has been identified, linguistic interpretation can be
built upon it, with constant attention to the name’s development and
alteration and to the formation of comparable name forms.

Admittedly, the possibility of multiple meanings—or the multiplicity of
possible explanations—does not always permit complete certainty, even
where family history appears to justify proposing an interpretation. In
any event, the purpose and goal of name research should not be the
interpretation of a name, but the history of the name. That history must
never examine an individual personal or *Familienname* in isolation. It
must study the name within the general processes of name formation and
development, taking factual, linguistic, chronological, regional, and
local circumstances into account.

The modern spelling of a name sometimes rests on accident. “Orthography”
in the modern sense did not yet exist, and dialect influence led to
further changes. Conversely, differing original forms could converge
during their development upon the same final form. Until well into the
nineteenth century, names were entered in documents and registers almost
never after consultation of documentary evidence, but according to oral
statements, often with numerous variants even within the same written
record.

It must also be remembered that, until the nineteenth century,
*Familiennamen* were by no means inherited only through the male line.
They were not infrequently transmitted through the female line in cases
of legitimate descent. In some instances, a *Familienname* was passed on
because of a service relationship. Succession to property or to a
business could be enough to transfer a name even without blood
relationship or formal adoption.

When no other suitable sources are available, researchers often attempt
to construct a family relationship from recurring “leading names.”

Such a relationship exists, however, in only a very small number of
cases. The term leading name is used when a grandfather’s *Rufname* is
repeatedly passed to a grandson, or when the firstborn sons in the
various branches of a dynastic or noble family each bear the same
*Rufname* inherited from the father. The existence of a leading-name
pattern should not be regarded as proven until evidence other than
onomastic facts—such as coats of arms or seals—confirms the supposition.

One must also guard against assuming that persons bearing the same name
are related. This applies not only to very common *Familiennamen*—Braun,
Becker, Fischer, Haase, Hoffmann, Krause, Krüger, Lehmann, Lange, Mayer,
Müller, Neumann, Richter, Schmidt, Schneider, Scholz, Schröder, Schulze,
Schwarz, Wagner, Weber, Weiß, Wolf, and Zimmermann—but generally also to
rarer *Familiennamen*. A genealogical connection among their bearers can
be demonstrated only through research supported by documentary sources.
The more extensive the family-history research and the broader its
historical foundation, the more securely the *Familienname* can be
interpreted.
